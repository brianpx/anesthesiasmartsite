Foundations, PICO, and the EQUATOR network

Appraisal is not a reading exercise. It ends in a decision you either change or keep, and the question you write at the start decides what the paper can settle.

Why appraisal changes a decision

The purpose is not to grade the paper. An appraisal that ends in “well-designed study” has not finished. It finishes when you can say what you would do differently on the next case, or say that you would do the same thing and now know why.

Reading for the decision also tells you when to stop. If the paper cannot change any choice you make, the methods do not matter. If it could change a choice, you read the methods closely enough to know whether to trust the number, and you read the population closely enough to know whether the number is about your patient.

That is the whole arc of a session: a question, a design, a result with its interval, and one sentence about what you will do. The order for a single paper walks the same arc for one article.

A vague question becomes a PICO

“Are TAP blocks worth doing?” is not a question. It has no comparator and no outcome, so no study can answer it, and two studies that disagree will both look relevant. A PICO forces the four things a study has to have fixed before it can produce a number, plus the one thing people leave off.

  • Population. Who was enrolled, including the operation and the setting, not just the diagnosis.
  • Intervention. What was done, in enough detail to repeat it. Drug, dose, timing, and technique.
  • Comparison. What it was done instead of.
  • Outcome. What was measured, and by whom.
  • Time. When the outcome was measured. This is the part that gets dropped, and it is often where two papers stop agreeing.

Two papers from the October session use the same block and answer different questions. Read the columns across, not down.

PICO for Grape 2021 compared with CLEVELAND 2026

Grape asks which of two local-anesthetic techniques to use for one operation. CLEVELAND asks whether local anesthetic in a routine preincision block beats saline injected the same way, in a mixed population. A favorable result in the first does not license the practice tested in the second, and a null result in the second does not overturn the first.

The comparator is the question

Most of the arguments in a journal club are not about statistics. They are about two people holding different comparators and using the same words.

Evered 2021 randomized a BIS target of 50 against a BIS target of 35, and found delirium in 19% versus 28%. ENGAGES randomized EEG-guided anesthesia against usual care, which mandated no target, and found 26% versus 23%. Same monitor, opposite-sounding conclusions, and no contradiction. Neither trial ran the other trial’s comparison.

So before you argue about whether the evidence supports a monitor, say what the alternative on offer is. If the choice in front of you is a lighter target versus a deeper one, you are in Evered. If it is a target versus no target, you are in ENGAGES. Name the comparator out loud and half the disagreement disappears.

The EQUATOR network

EQUATOR is a library of reporting guidelines, one per study type. Each is a checklist of what the authors should have put on the page. Knowing which guideline applies gives you a list of things to look for, and a fast way to notice something missing.

A reporting guideline is not a quality score. It tells you what should be on the page. It does not tell you whether the study was any good. Compliance with CONSORT means a trial was described well, and a well-described trial can still be a small unblinded trial with a soft outcome.

Judging the study itself is a separate job with separate tools: RoB 2 for randomized trials, ROBINS-I for nonrandomized studies of interventions, AMSTAR 2 for systematic reviews, PROBAST for prediction models. The relevant one appears in the part that covers its design.

At the table

  1. Say the decision first.

    In one sentence, before the abstract: what choice could this paper change?

  2. Write the study’s PICO, not yours.

    Fill in what the authors actually ran, including the time point. Then compare it with your question and name the gaps.

  3. Name the comparator out loud.

    Placebo, usual care, an active alternative, or a different dose of the same thing. These are four different papers.

  4. Name the design, and the guideline that goes with it.

    You now have a list of what should be on the page.

  5. Check the primary outcome against the registration.

    One primary outcome, declared in advance. A primary outcome that moved between the registry and the paper is a finding of its own.
