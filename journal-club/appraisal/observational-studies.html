Observational studies and STROBE

Without randomization, the groups differ for reasons, and the reasons are usually the reason the clinician chose the treatment. Appraising an observational study is mostly about naming those reasons and asking what the authors did about them.

What you give up, and what you get

You give up the one thing only randomization provides: groups that are alike on the variables nobody measured. Every observational estimate is therefore an association, with a plausible causal reading attached, and the appraisal is an argument about how much of the association is the treatment.

What you get in exchange is real. Large numbers, long follow-up, actual practice rather than trial conditions, outcomes rare enough that no trial will ever be powered for them, and exposures nobody would randomize. Those are not consolation prizes. A registry study of a complication that happens once in two thousand cases is the only study you are going to get.

A large database does not reduce confounding. It makes a confounded estimate precise. Ten million records will give you a tight confidence interval around the wrong number, and the tightness is often what convinces people.

Cohort, case-control, cross-sectional

Name the design before you read the result, because the design fixes which measures the study can legitimately report.

Observational designs compared
DesignDirectionWhat it can reportWhere it breaks
Cohort Exposure first, then follow forward for the outcome Incidence, risk ratio, hazard ratio, absolute risk Loss to follow-up, and confounding by the reason for the exposure
Case-control Outcome first, then look back for the exposure Odds ratio only. It cannot give you incidence How the controls were chosen, and recall of exposure
Cross-sectional Both measured at one time Prevalence, and associations Cannot establish which came first

Two practical notes. A case-control study that reports a risk ratio has reported something it cannot estimate, and that is worth catching. And a study labeled “retrospective cohort” on a hospital database is still a cohort: the question is whether the exposure was recorded before the outcome occurred, not when the investigator started looking.

Confounding

A confounder is a variable that is associated with the exposure, is an independent cause of the outcome, and is not simply a step on the path from the exposure to the outcome. All three conditions matter. Adjusting for something on the causal path removes part of the effect you were trying to measure.

A worked example we already know the answer to

Imagine a registry study of spinal versus general anesthesia for hip fracture. In practice the frailest patients, the ones with the worst cardiac disease, are often steered toward spinal. Frailty independently causes death. So a registry comparison will show a mortality difference that has a lot of frailty in it, and no amount of careful writing tells you how much.

REGAIN randomized that exact question. At 365 days there were 98 deaths in the spinal arm and 92 in the general arm, hazard ratio 1.08 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.81 to 1.44. Walking among survivors did not differ either. That is what the answer looks like when allocation is random, and it is the reference point for reading any registry estimate on the same question.

Confounding by indication

In perioperative data this is the dominant confounder and it rarely has a column in the dataset. The clinician chose the treatment for a reason, the reason involved the patient’s risk, and the risk drives the outcome. You cannot adjust for a clinical impression that was never written down.

What adjustment can and cannot do

Multivariable regression, propensity score matching, inverse probability weighting, and stratification all do the same job: they balance the confounders you measured. They do nothing about the ones you did not, and a propensity score is not more powerful than a regression, only differently presented. So the question to ask of any adjusted estimate is not which method was used. It is what was left out of the model.

Ask it specifically. ASA class is a coarse proxy for exactly the thing you worry about. Frailty, functional status, cognition, and the surgeon’s sense of how the case will go are usually absent. When a study reports that adjustment moved the estimate a long way, that tells you measured confounding was substantial, which is a reason to suspect the unmeasured kind is too.

Two tools exist for the residual. A negative control outcome, which the exposure could not plausibly cause, should show no effect. An E-value states how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the result away. Neither is common in the anesthesia literature, and both are worth noticing when they appear.

Selection and measurement

Who got into the dataset, and who left

Selection bias enters at entry and at exit. At entry, ask who is eligible to appear at all: a single-center cohort, patients with complete records, patients who survived long enough to be coded, patients whose insurance is in the claims database. At exit, ask who was lost, and whether being lost is related to the outcome.

Two named patterns are worth recognizing. Immortal time bias arises when a period during which the outcome could not occur is counted as exposed time, which makes the exposure look protective. The healthy-user effect arises when people who receive a preventive treatment are also the people who do everything else right.

In a case-control study, control selection is the whole design. Controls should come from the same population the cases came from, and should have been eligible to become cases. Hospital controls drawn from a different service are the classic failure.

Measurement

Ask how the exposure and the outcome were measured, and whether the accuracy could differ between groups. Nondifferential misclassification usually pulls the estimate toward no effect. Differential misclassification, where one group is measured more carefully, can push it either way, and it is the more dangerous of the two.

Administrative codes deserve particular suspicion, because they were generated for billing rather than for research. Delirium is the example close to hand. ENGAGES assessed delirium prospectively with a structured instrument on each of the first five postoperative days. A claims database finds only the delirium that someone bothered to code, which is a small and non-random subset. The two studies are using the same word for different measurements, and their rates are not comparable.

STROBE

STROBE is a 22-item checklist for reporting observational research, with versions for cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional designs. As in Part 1, it is a reporting standard and not a quality score.

Five of its items carry most of the appraisal weight, and they are the ones to check directly:

  • Eligibility criteria, and the sources and methods of participant selection.
  • How each exposure, outcome, confounder, and effect modifier was defined and measured.
  • What was done to address confounding, stated as a list of variables rather than a method name.
  • How missing data were handled.
  • The number of participants at each stage, and the reasons for non-participation.

For a judgment about the study rather than its reporting, the tool for nonrandomized studies of interventions is ROBINS-I, which asks about confounding and selection before the intervention, classification of the intervention, and then deviations, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Its useful discipline is that it makes you name the target trial the observational study is imitating.

Reading an observational estimate

Having named the problems, you still have to say something useful. Three questions help.

How large is the association, and is it large enough that residual confounding is an implausible explanation? A hazard ratio of 1.1 in a registry is within the range that unmeasured frailty could produce. A tenfold difference usually is not.

Is there a dose-response pattern, and is it in the right direction? Is the finding consistent across settings, and does it survive when different investigators adjust for different things?

And what is the mechanism? An association you can explain physiologically is not thereby true, but an association with no plausible mechanism and a small effect size in a large database is usually confounding.

Say it as an estimate with a size and a caveat, not as a fact and not as nothing. “In this cohort the association was X, adjusted for the following, and the main thing not adjusted for is Y” is a complete sentence and an honest one.

At the table

  1. Name the design, and check the measure matches it.

    A case-control study cannot report incidence or a risk ratio.

  2. Why did these patients get this treatment?

    The answer is the main confounder. Then ask whether it was measured.

  3. List what was adjusted for, then list what was not.

    The second list is the appraisal. Frailty, functional status, and clinical judgment are the usual absences.

  4. Who could get into this dataset, and who dropped out?

    Check for immortal time and for loss to follow-up related to the outcome.

  5. How was the outcome ascertained, and could that differ by group?

    A billing code is not a prospective assessment.

  6. Is the effect large enough to survive residual confounding?

    And is the interval narrow because the question was answered, or because the database was big?

  7. Is there a randomized trial on the same question?

    If there is, the observational study is context. If there is not, say why one does not exist.
