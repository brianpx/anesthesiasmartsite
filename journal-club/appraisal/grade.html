GRADE

Appraising one paper tells you about that paper. A decision needs a judgment across the whole body of evidence, made one outcome at a time. GRADE is a way to make that judgment explicit instead of leaving it in someone’s head.

From one study to a judgment

By the end of a session you have appraised two or three papers and someone asks the real question: so what should we do. Answering it means combining what you read with everything else known about the question, and saying how confident you are. That confidence is a judgment, and the useful thing about GRADE is that it makes the judgment structured, visible, and arguable.

The core idea is one sentence. Certainty is your confidence that the true effect is close enough to the estimate to support the decision you are about to make. Not whether the study was well conducted, and not whether the effect is large.

Certainty is per outcome

This is the part most often skipped, and it does the most work. A single review can support quite different certainty for its different outcomes, because the studies, the numbers, and the measurement quality differ between them.

In Grape, pain at rest at two hours was the primary outcome, pooled across the included trials with a reasonably tight interval. Nausea and vomiting is a different outcome, reported by fewer trials, measured less consistently, and with a wider interval. Treating them as one bundle called “analgesic benefit” loses the distinction that matters, which is that you are more confident about one than the other.

So a GRADE statement always has an outcome in it. “Moderate certainty for delirium” is a sentence. “Moderate-quality evidence” is not.

The four levels

GRADE certainty levels
LevelWhat it asserts
HighThe true effect is very likely close to the estimate. Further research is unlikely to change the picture
ModerateThe true effect is probably close to the estimate, but it could be substantially different
LowThe true effect may be substantially different from the estimate
Very lowThe estimate is barely more than a guess

High certainty does not mean a large effect. You can have high certainty that a treatment does almost nothing, and that is one of the most useful results a body of evidence can produce.

Where you start, and five reasons to go down

Randomized trials start at high. Observational studies start at low, for the reasons in Part 3. Then five domains can each pull the rating down by one level, or by two when the problem is severe.

The five reasons to downgrade certainty
DomainThe questionFrom our sessions
Risk of bias Are the studies at serious risk of bias on this outcome? ENGAGES could not blind the anesthesia clinician, but the delirium assessors were blinded and the sensitivity analyses held, so that alone is weak grounds for downgrading. A body of small unblinded block trials with self-reported pain is a stronger case
Imprecision Does the interval cross a threshold that would change the decision? CLEVELAND’s liposomal-versus-saline interval runs from a 40% opioid reduction to a 24% increase. That crosses the decision point in both directions. Downgrade
Inconsistency Do the studies disagree, in a way the review cannot explain? Check whether the disagreement is real or is two different comparators. Evered and ENGAGES look inconsistent and are not, because they ran different trials
Indirectness Is this the population, intervention, comparator, and outcome you care about? REGAIN enrolled hip fracture. Using it for elective total hip arthroplasty on an ERAS pathway is indirect on both population and comparator
Publication bias Is it likely that studies exist which were never published? Suspect it when the evidence is a set of small positive industry-funded trials and no large neutral one

Two notes on doing this honestly. Imprecision is judged against a threshold you have to name out loud, which is why the minimal clinically important difference keeps coming up. And you do not downgrade for a problem twice: if an outcome is imprecise because it was only measured in two small trials, that is imprecision, not imprecision plus risk of bias.

Two reasons to go up

Rating up applies to observational evidence and it is uncommon. A very large effect, of the kind residual confounding could not plausibly produce, can raise certainty. So can a clear dose-response gradient. There is a third, rarely invoked, where all the plausible unmeasured confounding would have pushed the result in the opposite direction from the one observed.

You do not rate a randomized trial up, because it started at the top.

Certainty is not strength

These are separate, and conflating them causes real arguments. Certainty is about the estimate. The strength of a recommendation weighs the certainty together with the size of the benefit, the size and nature of the harms, how much patients vary in what they value, and what it costs in drug, time, staffing, and attention.

So low certainty can still support a conditional recommendation, when the intervention is cheap, safe, and plausibly helpful. And high certainty of a tiny benefit compels nothing at all. Grape is close to that case: a real difference of 0.7 points on a 0 to 10 pain scale is a finding you can be fairly confident about and still decide not to act on.

When a recommendation is conditional, the useful follow-up question at journal club is which patient would tip it. That is where the vignette in the second half of the session earns its place.

Saying it out loud

A GRADE-shaped sentence has four parts: certainty, outcome, population, and the reason for the rating. Practice the shape on the papers you already know.

“Moderate certainty that a BIS target of 50 rather than 35 reduces postoperative delirium in older adults having major noncardiac surgery: 19% versus 28% in a single randomized trial, downgraded for imprecision and for resting on one trial.”

“Low to moderate certainty that a routine preincision four-quadrant TAP block does not meaningfully reduce opioid use in mixed major abdominal surgery: one randomized trial, with an interval from a 40% reduction to a 24% increase. Downgraded for imprecision.”

“Moderate to high certainty that spinal and general anesthesia give similar one-year survival after hip fracture surgery in previously ambulatory adults: hazard ratio 1.08, 95% CI 0.81 to 1.44. This says nothing about elective total hip arthroplasty, where the evidence is indirect.”

Those are worked examples for practice, written to show the shape of the sentence. They are not published GRADE assessments, and a formal rating on any of these questions would be done by a panel against a named threshold. Expect to defend your rating and expect someone to argue for one level higher or lower. That argument is the useful part.

At the table

  1. Pick the outcome first.

    Rate one outcome at a time, and say which one.

  2. Set the starting point.

    Randomized, start high. Observational, start low.

  3. Name your threshold before judging imprecision.

    The difference you would act on, in the units of the outcome.

  4. Walk the five domains and say which ones you downgraded for.

    Risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, publication bias. Do not double-count one problem.

  5. Check that apparent inconsistency is not two different questions.

    Different comparators produce different answers without either being wrong.

  6. State certainty and the recommendation separately.

    Then say what would change your mind, and what patient would tip it.
