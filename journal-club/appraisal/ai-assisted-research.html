AI-assisted research

A model can search, screen, extract, and draft. It cannot be accountable for a sentence. That single asymmetry decides which steps you can delegate and which ones you still do by hand.

The work that does not transfer

Language models are good at the parts of research that look like handling text: finding candidates, summarizing, restructuring, tightening prose. They are unreliable at the part that matters most in appraisal, which is whether a specific claim is true of a specific paper.

The failure mode is not that models are often wrong. It is that a model which is confident and wrong reads exactly like a model which is confident and right. There is no tell. Fluency is not evidence, and the smoothness of a summary carries no information about its accuracy. That is why every use below comes with a check performed against the paper itself.

The practical rule for a journal club: you may use a model to decide what to read. You may not use it to decide what a paper says.

Task by task, with the check each one needs

Uses of a model in research, and the verification each requires
TaskGenuinely useful forWhat you must verify
Finding papers Synonyms you would not have thought of, a starting set, orienting in an unfamiliar field That every citation exists. Open the DOI or the PMID, not the title. Fabricated and subtly mismatched references are the signature failure
Building a search strategy MeSH terms, Boolean structure, a first draft of the string Run it yourself in PubMed or Embase and record the exact string and the date. A search you did not execute is not a search
Screening First-pass triage of titles and abstracts against explicit criteria A human reviews every exclusion. If a model made exclusion decisions in a systematic review, you cannot claim two independent human screeners, and you have to report what the model did
Extracting numbers Pulling effect sizes and intervals into a table quickly Every number against the paper, including the sign and both ends of the interval. Transposed limits and dropped minus signs are common and quiet
Summarizing a paper Orientation before you read, and a check on your own reading afterward Read the paper. A summary cannot tell you whether allocation was concealed, and it will flatten the comparator
Drafting Structure, tightening, a first attempt at a BLUF you then rewrite Every sentence, because you are now the author of it. Check that no claim arrived without a source
Statistics and code Explaining a method, writing analysis code you will inspect The method against a textbook, and the code against a worked example with a known answer

The distinction a summary is most likely to lose is the comparator, which is also the distinction appraisal turns on. Ask a model about TAP blocks and it will happily blend Grape and CLEVELAND into one claim about whether TAP blocks work. Those are two questions with two different control groups, as Part 1 lays out, and blending them produces a sentence that is wrong about both.

Check these every time

  1. Does the citation exist?

    Resolve the DOI or the PMID. A plausible author list, journal, and year assembled around a paper that was never written is the most common failure, and it survives a search by title because the title sounds right.

  2. Does the paper say what the summary said?

    Check the population, the comparator, the outcome, and the time point specifically. Those four are where a summary drifts.

  3. Are the numbers right?

    Point estimate, both interval limits, the direction, and the units. Read them off the paper, not off the summary of the paper.

  4. What is missing?

    A model summarizes what it retrieved or remembers, which is not the literature. Ask specifically for the strongest evidence against the claim, then go and check whether that evidence exists too.

  5. Is the search reproducible?

    Database, full string, date run, and number of records. Without those you have a reading list, not a search.

  6. Whose words are these?

    Generated text you have not rewritten and cannot source is a problem of authorship, not of style.

  7. What did you put in the prompt?

    No protected health information and no unpublished data belong in a tool that has not been approved for them.

A reproducible search

If a model helped build the search, the record has to be able to stand without it. Keep four things: the database and interface, the complete search string including field tags and limits, the date the search was run, and the number of records returned at each step. That is what makes the search a method rather than an anecdote, and it is what Part 4 asks you to look for in someone else’s review.

Tools that retrieve live sources and show you the retrieved passages are easier to verify than a model answering from memory, because you can open what it opened. That makes verification faster. It does not make it optional, and a quoted passage can still be attached to the wrong claim.

Disclosure and what goes in the prompt

A model cannot be an author. Authorship carries accountability for the work and the ability to approve the final version, and a tool can do neither. Journals expect you to disclose what you used and what it did, typically in the methods or the acknowledgments, and the requirements differ between journals. Check the instructions for the journal you are actually submitting to before you write the statement.

On inputs, treat any prompt as a disclosure. Patient identifiers, unpublished data from a colleague, and text under review elsewhere do not go into a general-purpose tool. Institutional approval for a specific tool is about where the data goes, and it is not transferable to whichever tool is open in another tab.

Use a model to get to the papers faster. Do the appraisal yourself. The sentence you say at the table is yours, and so is the error in it.

At the table

  1. Say where the model was used.

    Search, screening, extraction, drafting. One sentence, not a confession.

  2. Show that you opened the papers.

    Quote a number off the page, with its interval.

  3. Name the comparator yourself.

    This is the thing a summary gets wrong, so it is the thing to state from the paper.

  4. Bring the search string if you built one.

    Database, string, date, record count.

  5. Bring the strongest paper against your BLUF.

    If you could not find one, say how you looked.
